To address this challenge, we present ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull, a resource-efficient and deterministic approach that identifies malicious URLs through the formation of mathematically defined convex hull perimeters across multiple orthogonal feature domains (structural content entropy, hyperlink graph topography, semantic token distribution). Keywords: phishing detection; malicious URLs; convex hull; geometric anomaly detection; explainable security. 1 Introduction The phishing site can be described as a website that mimics the websites of well-known organizations. It is intended to trick users into surrendering their login information and other sensitive data. The use of such websites as a launching pad for cyber attacks means that phishing pages have become more and more sophisticated. The use of advanced phishing frameworks is an emerging and serious threat to the worldwide network systems. Today’s cyber attackers have moved beyond simple fake domain names. Instead, they use automated phishing generation pipelines that can produce adaptive malicious URLs with advanced evasion techniques such as highly obfuscated HTML structures [ 1 ] , randomized routing mechanisms [ 2 ] , and irregular entropy distributions in web content [ 3 ] . Industry reports from recent years illustrate the scale and impact of these threats. The Anti-Phishing Working Group (APWG) has reported more than one million phishing campaigns each quarter [ 4 ] . The FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) reports billions of dollars in annual losses due to phishing and business-email-compromise incidents [ 5 ] . By design, these adversaries mimic legitimate site behavior while constantly changing observable signatures, severely degrading the effectiveness of traditional blacklist and signature-based detection systems [ 6 ] . This has resulted in the emergence of novel, previously unseen phishing campaigns that threaten the operational integrity of large-scale Internet infrastructures and critical cyber-physical systems. Therefore, it is vital to adopt a radical new approach from rule-based methods to structural anomaly detection processes in response to such a daunting situation. While the academic literature provides a variety of analytical countermeasures to address these challenges, all existing paradigms suffer from fundamental weaknesses. To deal with such challenges, cybersecurity professionals have introduced different analytical methods [ 7 ] , but all of them involve essential weaknesses. Traditional statistical and probabilistic algorithms are usually ineffective since they operate under fixed constraints regarding past data distribution patterns [ 8 ] ; these systems are prone to quickly deteriorate in case if the attack causes changes to the nature of the signature left by the adversary. Graph-theoretic and heuristic methods, in turn, try to understand the network connections [ 9 ] and structure of the content [ 10 ] , but they are costly and can easily be interfered with by "noise," such as injecting innocent keywords and dead links by the attacker. Thus, DL systems are widely used because of their high performance in classifying different threats [ 11 ] . Nevertheless, the modern neural nets also pose certain issues since they are unable to provide deterministic explanations (see the table 1 ) . Namely, their training process is quite demanding in terms of computer power, and they are based on obscure parameter weights that do not allow one to determine why the algorithm classified certain domains as malicious in mathematical terms [ 12 ] . ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull does not model dynamic adversarial behaviors in a constant manner, but instead uses an explicit verification paradigm, requiring each web request to meet pre-defined mathematical boundary conditions to be legitimate. The proposed framework uses convex geometric representations to give mathematically provable safe regions that represent predictable benign web traffic, enabling deterministic isolation of anomalous requests that stray outside the learned spatial boundaries. To this end, ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull maps high-variance web attributes such as aggregate content entropy, semantic keyword distributions and hyperlink topology ratios into a multi-dimensional coordinate space. The framework then constructs several independent geometric “Vaults” for cross-space verification using QuickHull [ 13 ] -based convex boundary generation. Thus, even if a sophisticated phishing payload succeeds in mimicking one structural property of legitimate traffic, it is mathematically bound to violate the orthogonal constraints of another Vault during spatial containment verification [ 6 ] . Experiments on benchmark phishing datasets demonstrate that the proposed multi-tier geometric framework achieves 92% detection accuracy with significantly lower computational overhead compared to deep learning alternatives. However, applying the framework in practice poses several challenges, including the computational cost of mapping high-dimensional spaces and the need to periodically re-calibrate convex boundaries to account for the evolving topology of legitimate web infrastructures over time. Expanding upon the above empirical results, this study will tackle the weaknesses inherent to probabilistic threat detection by way of the following fundamental innovations: • We propose ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull, a deterministic geometric threat-isolation framework, leveraging Convex Hulls to derive mathematically verifiable spatial boundaries for legitimate web traffic. • A multi-tier cross-reference geometric verification strategy for detecting adversarial phishing payloads across multiple orthogonal feature spaces, and achieving 92% detection accuracy with a significantly lower computational overhead than conventional deep learning-based approaches. Most importantly, the proposed geometric framework relies on the use of mathematically verifiable spatial representations, rather than on opaque hidden weight optimization, thus the decision-making process is inherently interpretable and computationally lightweight. Moreover, the deterministic nature of the framework reduces the need for extensive heuristic tuning, making it more suitable for scalable and automated threat analysis environments. Contemporary research into malicious URLs has relied significantly on natural language processing (NLP) and TF-IDF extraction to determine the semantics of the web content [ 3 , 14 ] . On that premise, various scholars have made use of ensemble learning and SVM techniques to predict malicious URLs through structural entropy and lexical attributes [ 11 ] . Recent studies have also looked into hybrid and ensemble-driven cybersecurity frameworks that combine statistical filtering, graph analytics and machine learning classifiers to achieve better robustness against adversarial manipulation and feature obfuscation [ 15 ] . The goal of these approaches is to combine the interpretability of structured analytical methods with the adaptive learning capabilities of modern classifiers [ 16 ] . However, while these hybrid systems achieve better detection performance, they still largely rely on probabilistic inference mechanisms and often inherit the computational overhead and explainability limitations associated with conventional machine learning models. In recent times, deep neural networks [ 15 , 7 ] and graph-based techniques [ 9 , 10 ] have been extensively explored. These paradigms exhibit exceptional ability to detect hyperlink connections and extract latent non-linear features, thereby enhancing classification performance. However, despite these systems demonstrating impressive abilities, the contemporary literature identifies their significant drawbacks. For one, deep learning models require considerable amounts of time for training alongside intensive computer memory space [ 12 ] ; hence, these systems cannot function efficiently within edge devices that are limited by computing power and storage capacity. Moreover, since such systems utilize probabilistic parameters for prediction rather than spatial rules, they fail to provide sufficient transparency on why a particular anomaly triggered an alarm [ 16 ] . This precise problem of lacking mathematical proof has compelled scholars in other critical areas to explore geometric models. Deep learning frameworks [ 12 ] have computational constraints, and classical statistical and linear dimensionality-reduction approaches have also exhibited important limitations in modern web-based threat environments [ 6 ] . Popular machine learning techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) assume very strong assumptions on linear separability and normally distributed feature spaces [ 8 ] . However, modern phishing ecosystems exhibit strongly non-linear characteristics with irregular entropy distributions [ 3 ] , semantic anomalies and dynamically evolving hyperlink relationships [ 9 , 10 ] . Thus, simple linear transformation methods often do not yield reliable decision boundaries for complex adversarial web traffic patterns. This practical problem in using linear classifiers, exacerbated by the "black box" nature of deep neural networks [ 15 , 7 , 16 ] , has resulted in an important operational gap in the field of threat identification in real time. For applications in which predictability and transparency are key, scientists studying defensive mechanisms in physical spaces like autonomous UAV swarm tracking, missile trajectory representation, and radar border mapping have effectively eliminated probabilistic guesswork by employing computational geometry [ 13 ] . In such high-stakes areas of study, N-dimensional Convex Hulls form inflexible and unforgiving spatial borders surrounding known objects. Nevertheless, although these robust mathematical concepts have found widespread use in the analysis of physical battlefields, they have seen little application in cybersecurity to date. Consequently, one major limitation is that no deterministic and geometrically meaningful cybersecurity approach has been developed yet, which can effectively separate malicious web traffic without depending solely on probabilistic learning models. ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull is designed precisely for this purpose. This paper solves the problem by applying topological principles to map non-linear traits of the cyber domain into individual geometric Vaults [ 6 ] . 3 Proposed Model In this section, we begin by describing the seed dataset utilized in this work. 3.1 Benchmark Dataset Description In order to test the ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull algorithm empirically, we used a data set based on the Web Page Phishing Detection Dataset [ 2 ] . An initial seed of 11,430 URL samples, each representing 91 features, was put through an elaborate preprocessing procedure. In the end, the highly curated set consisted of 769 legitimate and 46 phishing URL samples, containing only those whose hyperlinks and semantic data could be completely extracted [ 1 ] . The drastic reduction in the size of the dataset was mainly due to the strict preprocessing constraints needed for deterministic geometric modelling. A significant proportion of the original URLs contained incomplete hyperlink structures, broken semantic metadata, inaccessible embedded resources, or partially missing entropy-related attributes, rendering them unsuitable for reliable high-dimensional spatial mapping. In the final experimental study, we considered only the samples with completely extractable and structurally valid feature spaces, because the proposed convex-boundary framework relies on mathematically consistent feature representations in all dimensions. The final dataset exhibits a very severe class imbalance between legitimate and phishing samples. This distribution was intentionally preserved. Operationally, this 16:1 ratio is an accurate approximation of real web environments and enables a more rigorous evaluation of the framework under real-world anomaly-isolation conditions. More important mathematically, this imbalance does not require any synthetic correction (like SMOTE). In contrast to probabilistic classifiers that minimize frequency-based loss functions, thus being sensitive to class imbalance, the ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull framework depends only on spatial containment. A Convex Hull is defined only by the coordinates (vertices) of the outer boundary of the valid data cluster. Thus, the safe zone design is strictly invariant to the frequency distribution of malicious samples; hence, oversampling methods are mathematically irrelevant in the process of forming geometric boundaries. The final data set was presented in the form of a JSON object, containing deep attributes such as links distribution, term frequencies (TF-IDF) [ 14 ] , and explicit Shannon entropy [ 3 ] .
A cursory analysis of the data indicated specific architectural differences, supporting our choice of a geometric technique. Indeed, legitimate URLs create intricate graph structures [ 9 ] , featuring intensive navigation within the site and numerous outbound links. Moreover, the semantics used in their descriptions include terms related to information (such as "institute" and "academic") and a high amount of structural entropy, implying diverse HTML content.
However, malicious domains have a very sparse and isolated network structure for hyperlinks that is characteristic of a domain meant only for credential harvesting [ 10 ] . The semantic words in their case consist of an extremely high number of urgency-based words (such as login, secure, bank), leading to entropy reduction. Through the exploitation of such great differences in hyper-link density, intention, and entropy, we achieve a highly discriminating multi-dimensional basis in which our algorithm is evaluated. 3.2 Probabilistic Vault Selection & Spatial Mapping Unlike in heuristic approaches where the features chosen for use are rather arbitrarily picked, ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull uses a mathematical method that determines the best coordinate axes to use for geometric bounding through a probabilistic approach [ 8 ] . Let the set of features extracted be represented as F = { f 1 , f 2 , … , f 91 } F=\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{91}\} . For each such feature f i f_{i} , the probabilities P ⁡ ( f i ∣ Legitimate ) P(f_{i}\mid\text{Legitimate}) and P ⁡ ( f i ∣ Malicious ) P(f_{i}\mid\text{Malicious}) are computed. Separability measure for every feature was calculated using the following normalized probabilistic distance metric: S ⁡ ( f i ) = | μ L ​ ( f i ) − μ M ​ ( f i ) | σ L ​ ( f i ) + σ M ​ ( f i ) S(f_{i})=\frac{\left|\mu_{L}(f_{i})-\mu_{M}(f_{i})\right|}{\sigma_{L}(f_{i})+\sigma_{M}(f_{i})} where μ L ​ ( f i ) \mu_{L}(f_{i}) and μ M ​ ( f i ) \mu_{M}(f_{i}) are the means for feature f i f_{i} in the legitimate and malicious sample sets, and σ L ​ ( f i ) \sigma_{L}(f_{i}) and σ M ​ ( f i ) \sigma_{M}(f_{i}) are their respective standard deviations. The features with high separability scores surpass the predefined threshold, thus ensuring no spatial overlap among the classes.
The best performing pairs are selected and mapped in three different Vaults into X and Y axes. The three Vaults chosen are representative of different behavioural dimensions of legitimate web infrastructure and adversarial traffic patterns, as illustrated in Figure 7 . Specifically, the proposed framework constructs (i) a Structural Entropy Vault for the randomness and code-complexity analysis, (ii) a Semantic Keyword Vault for the linguistic and intent-based analysis, and (iii) a Hyperlink Graph Vault for the topological connectivity analysis. These three geometric subspaces together provide a mutually complementary multidimensional isolation of phishing behaviour in structural, semantic and network-topological terms. All candidate feature pairs were carefully assessed using the normalized separability metric described above. A total of 46 independent geometric Vault combinations were created and ranked according to their intra-class separability and the strength of their geometric isolation. The three Vaults selected in this work correspond to the best performing combinations of feature-pairs that consistently showed maximal spatial separation of legitimate and malicious traffic distributions while maintaining stable multidimensional boundary formation. On the other hand, 20% of the total traffic data that was densely populated with previously unseen attacks was designated for intersection testing (testing). The entire geometric framework was implemented using the Python version 3.12. The high-dimensional processing of the 91-dimensional vector datasets made use of Pandas and NumPy libraries. The key Convex Hull boundaries were estimated using the QuickHull algorithm based on the SciPy Spatial library [ 13 ] . Refraining from any complex GPU-based deep learning neural networks [ 15 ] , all testing was performed using a simple CPU configuration (Intel Core i7 and 16 GB RAM) [ 12 ] . Topological validation used Matplotlib to visualize the multi-vault 2D perimeters and Plotly for plotting the 3D projections of the Convex Hull boundaries in space. In order to create a rigid baseline comparison, the implementation of traditional and modern machine learning classifiers was added to the geometric framework. We also implemented Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) using the scikit-learn library. Finally, XGBoost architecture was implemented using the native xgboost Python framework. To ensure a fair comparison, all machine learning baselines were hyper-parameter tuned using Grid Search (maximum tree depth, learning rate, kernel settings, etc.) to maximize their predictive ability with respect to the class imbalance in the dataset. The experimental assessment was further validated by employing stratified 5-fold cross-validation to increase statistical validity and minimize sampling bias. The partitions maintained the same ratio of legit to phishing samples in all five validation folds, allowing for accurate construction of geometrical boundaries and constant behavior for anomaly isolation. The measured statistics reflect the averaged results from all validation folds. 4 Results & Discussion In this section,we summarise the results of our dataset enhancement and examine their impact on malicious URL detection. 4.1 Dataset Preprocessing & Vault Optimization Overview In an effort to scientifically validate our ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull structure, our first step involved analyzing the optimal selection of high dimensional input data, which should form the basis of our defensive perimeter [ 2 ] . Instead of using any arbitrary selection of data points, the algorithm implemented a scientific pruning stage where it computed the probabilities of each class conditional attribute for the data at hand [ 8 ] . The goal of this stage was not only to reduce the number of dimensions but also to select those attributes that were mathematically different from one another. Through the calculation of variance distances between all permutations, the algorithm selected those feature pairs that exceeded the separability requirement. In effect, we reduced our massive feature space to 46 highly differentiating coordinate pairs [ 6 ] .

The rationale behind such behavior lies in an unsolvable dilemma that is inherent to any hostile campaign. Any attacker will resort to using either of two extremes: highly sparse HTML framework, tailored exclusively to credential harvesting with little regard for complexity, or extremely obfuscated JavaScript code that is aimed at evading traditional signature filters [ 1 ] . In both cases, the informational entropy of the page is dramatically distorted [ 3 ] . As shown in Figure 7 (left), authentic web infrastructure possesses a consistent, natural entropy signature. All legitimate requests fall into a tight, predictable cluster of highly dense variance (safe zone). In contrast to this, all adversary-based payloads dramatically diverge from the perimeter of trust. If the payload is sparse enough, then it will possess predictable uniformity; otherwise, it will demonstrate high cryptography-driven randomness. Yet, the true value of this technique does not lie in precision alone; rather, it lies in computational efficiency and the practicality of implementation at the network edge. 4.4.1 The Fallacy of Linear Baselines The abysmal performance of the PCA baseline (at only 32% precision) empirically disproves the so-called "Linear Fallacy" in contemporary cybersecurity [ 6 ] . Classic linear classifiers assume that threat vectors and benign flows can be discriminated using an evenly spaced hyperplane in the feature space. Section 4.3 discusses the results of our topological analysis and shows that state-of-the-art phishing attacks are designed to intersect the spatial coordinates of legitimate traffic. By dropping the idea of linear separation and employing multiple convex hulls [ 13 ] , our technique succeeds in separating the intersections and isolating attacks that can copy individual parameters but not the entire shape of benign flow vectors. 4.4.2 Computational Economics for Line-Rate Edge Deployment One major problem with current AI-based threat detection solutions such as DNNs and LLMs [ 15 ] is the sheer amount of computational power that these algorithms need to operate. Although effective, these AI models require substantial GPU acceleration and memory bandwidth, making them practically impossible to deploy on an edge device such as local routers, IoT gateways, and even hardware firewalls [ 12 ] . This makes it necessary to route network traffic through cloud servers, thus introducing latency into the threat detection process. The ℂ \mathbb{C} yber ℍ \mathbb{H} ull method effectively solves this problem by using mathematical principles. Firstly, the calculation of the geometrical vault using QuickHull [ 13 ] requires only 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log n) time complexity. Furthermore, checking whether an incoming network packet is indeed malicious becomes simply a matter of checking "point-in-polyhedron" mathematically [ 6 ] . After the mathematical boundaries of the vaults are set through the training phase, detection of the incoming previously unseen threat does not involve any computationally expensive processes like matrix multiplications or neural net predictions. All that needs to be done is to check if the multi-dimensional coordinates of the incoming payload lie within or outside the mathematical equations for the secure zone. It is a straightforward task that is completed in microseconds. As such, the solution can be implemented on the edge silicon with minimal processing power involved [ 12 ] . We also performed runtime benchmark experiments on all the baseline models evaluated in the same CPU-only execution environment to quantitatively verify the feasibility of edge-deployment for our proposed framework. The goal of this analysis was to estimate the practical computational overhead for real-time malicious traffic detection at the edge of the network. The inference latency, average memory consumption, and relative CPU usage are obtained during the live packet classification on an Intel Core i7 (16 GB of RAM). The benchmark results showed a great computational advantage of the proposed geometric framework. Since CyberHull architecture only performs deterministic point-in-polyhedron boundary verification at inference time, runtime overhead remained significantly lower compared to probabilistic machine learning approaches which require repeated matrix operations, kernel evaluations or ensemble tree traversals. In particular, the proposed framework achieved a consistent inference latency at the microsecond level, and used significantly less memory resources than XGBoost and Random Forest architectures. First, due to the strict mathematics involved in creating the convex hulls, the entire approach depends on the quality of the traffic used during the baseline training [ 2 ] . Hence, if the legitimate baseline traffic is compromised, then the geometrical perimeters are automatically going to rely on malicious shapes. Another important limitation is the highly curated nature of the experimental data set. The mandatory strict preprocessing to ensure mathematically consistent multidimensional feature extraction inevitably led to a loss of usable samples, limiting the diversity of observable web behaviours during geometric boundary establishment. Therefore, although the proposed framework performed robustly in the tested environment, more large-scale validation on continuously evolving real-world traffic distributions is still needed to fully assess the long-term generalization capability. Future work will therefore consist in designing adaptive perimeter refinement mechanisms that are able to dynamically update the geometric boundaries in the presence of legitimate behavioral drift and the appearance of new attack patterns. Secondly, as corporations change, they develop new sub-domains, and page topologies become more advanced than before. As such, the geometric perimeters will undergo "topological decay" over time and become more likely to generate false positives because legitimate traffic may slowly move out of its mathematical boundaries [ 6 ] . Going forward, the next step of this research involves incorporating the Federated Learning (FL) feedback cycle into the process, enabling edge nodes to dynamically adjust the local geometric perimeter according to changes in global legitimate traffic [ 12 ] . In this work we present the CyberHull framework, a multidimensional geometric threat-isolation architecture able to construct non-linear safe perimeters around legitimate network behaviour using Convex Hull boundary generation. The results from the experimental evaluation demonstrated that the proposed framework attained 92.0% global previously unseen interception accuracy, with low false positive behavior and significantly lower computational overhead than the conventional machine learning baselines such as PCA, SVM, Random Forest and XGBoost. Our results also show that deterministic geometric containment enables efficient real-time threat isolation with lightweight mathematical boundary verification, making the architecture suitable for practical line-rate deployment at the network edge. Besides detection accuracy, the proposed framework provides a new perspective for cybersecurity intelligence based on topological behaviour modelling instead of probabilistic prediction. The architecture, through the use of multiple independently optimized geometric vaults, was able to quarantine sophisticated adversarial payloads which attempted to mimic legitimate structural characteristics, but still contained hidden semantic, informational or topological inconsistencies. Moreover, the experimental runtime analysis proved the feasibility of the implementation of the framework in lightweight edge settings where the computationally infeasible deep learning architectures are not suitable due to latency, memory, and power constraints. The future work will be focused on extending the adaptability and long-term resilience of the proposed geometric architecture to the continuously evolving network environments. One of the major directions is the development of an Automated Geometric Pipeline that dynamically regenerates vault boundaries as legitimate corporate infrastructures change over time. Future work will explore adaptive perimeter refinement strategies based on Federated Learning (FL) protocols, where distributed edge nodes share geometric intelligence in a collaborative way without disclosing sensitive payload information. Large-scale validation across heterogeneous real-world traffic distributions, encrypted communication channels and multimodal behavioural features will also be needed to fully evaluate the generalization capability of the geometric threat isolation under highly dynamic adversarial conditions. Finally, the implementation of geometric cybersecurity frameworks creates some ethical and security concerns. Because the proposed model depends on mathematically constructed boundaries of behavior, it is possible that adversaries may try to infer or approximate legitimate geometric distributions in a step-by-step manner by using adaptive probing attacks. Similarly, Federated Learning based decentralized perimeter sharing mechanisms may be prone to poisoning attempts to corrupt the trusted geometric space. Future implementations will thus need to include secure perimeter synchronization, privacy-preserving geometric aggregation and adversarial robustness validation to ensure that collaborative defensive infrastructures do not become vectors of exploitation themselves. However, the proposed CyberHull framework demonstrates that multidimensional geometric containment is a promising and computationally sustainable direction for next generation threat defense systems despite these challenges.
